# Montréal and Toronto Home claim drivers

## What external evidence can explain a Montréal–Toronto water-claim difference?

### Takeaway

Public sources establish credible, city-specific pathways for basement flooding and sewer backup in both cities. They do not establish that Montréal has more insured Home claims than Toronto, nor explain any difference in the supplied portfolio; the data need postal-area, peril-cause, property, exposure, coverage, and event-date detail before testing these mechanisms.

### Cited Findings
- Montréal identifies intense rain, rapid street-water accumulation, sewer overload and sewer backup as property-flood pathways; its guidance specifically notes basement vulnerabilities, low-lying areas, building entry points, check valves and sump pumps. The city also describes its territory as highly mineralized/impervious and says intense rain can overwhelm sewers and accumulate on streets. [Montréal heavy-rain protection](https://montreal.ca/en/articles/protecting-your-building-heavy-rains-68382); [Montréal flood overview](https://montreal.ca/en/topics/flooding?arrondissement=OUT); [Montréal heavy-rain action plan](https://montreal.ca/articles/des-mesures-pour-faire-face-aux-fortes-pluies-et-contrer-les-inondations-68385)
- Toronto lists heavy rainfall/snowmelt, overloaded sewers, groundwater/stormwater infiltration, foundation cracks, grading, foundation-drain failure, sump-pump failure, eavestrough/downspout issues and sewer-line blockage as basement-flood causes. The city reports completing environmental assessments in 67 areas during 2006–2024 to identify basement/road-surface flood causes and mitigation. [Toronto basement flooding](https://www.toronto.ca/services-payments/water-environment/managing-rain-melted-snow/basement-flooding/); [Toronto protection program](https://www.toronto.ca/services-payments/water-environment/managing-rain-melted-snow/basement-flooding/basement-flooding-protection-program/)
- Environment and Climate Change Canada’s 2026 assessment says total annual precipitation has increased and is projected to continue increasing (high confidence); observed changes in heavy-precipitation frequency/intensity have lower confidence, while future increases are high confidence. It says extreme precipitation increases potential future flash flooding, including in urban areas. National-scale findings are not a Montréal-versus-Toronto comparison. [Canada’s Changing Climate Report 2026, Chapter 5](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/science-technology/changing-climate-report-2026/cccr-chapter-5-en.html)
- The 2026 federal report distinguishes pluvial, fluvial, groundwater and coastal flood mechanisms and cautions that insurance coverage depends on endorsements and flood type; sewer backup and overland flooding may require different coverage. Therefore, a “Water Damage” label alone cannot identify the hazard or the insured loss mechanism. [Canada’s Changing Climate Report 2026, Chapter 5](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/science-technology/changing-climate-report-2026/cccr-chapter-5-en.html)
- Statistics Canada’s 2026 analysis says insured flood losses are a major national driver and reports $9 billion paid for flooding over the prior 10 years, nearly half in 2024. It also notes replacement costs/building inputs and wages matter to Home insurance costs. Those national figures cannot attribute either this dataset’s claims or city differences. [Statistics Canada, Extreme weather impacts on consumers and insurers, 2019–2025](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11-621-m/11-621-m2026007-eng.htm)

### Inferences
- Montréal-vs-Toronto hypotheses worth testing: storm-day clustering; sewer backup vs overland vs groundwater vs internal plumbing; neighborhood-level imperviousness/low points; basement prevalence/use; backwater valve/sump protection; property age/type; and differences in endorsements, limits, deductibles, and claims coding. These are hypotheses suggested by local hazard pathways, not demonstrated portfolio causes.
- Join claims to insured postal code or geocode and verified loss cause, then align each loss date with local hourly/daily precipitation and snowmelt conditions. Compare claim frequency per insured property-year and severity per claim, after matching coverage, limits, deductible, property type/age, and policy tenure. City labels are too coarse for the local mechanisms identified by both municipalities.

### Gaps
- No retrieved source supplies a harmonized, insured-exposure-adjusted Montréal-versus-Toronto Home claim frequency/severity comparison for the dataset period.
- No policy-level postal/FSA, property attributes, coverage endorsement, deductible-at-loss, water-cause subtype, event identifier, or earned exposure was provided, so city-level water hypotheses cannot be verified.
- ECCC provides historical station observations and climate normals, but this portfolio has only city-level locations and no defined weather-matching method; weather matching would need station/grid selection and event windows. [ECCC Historical Climate Data](https://climate.weather.gc.ca/)

## Could theft or fire differences explain claim patterns?

### Takeaway

Public crime statistics can suggest an external check for theft, but broad police-reported crime rates are not insured-home theft claim rates. External sources support analyzing theft/fire separately by claim subtype and exposure; they do not justify treating a city or demographic group as the cause.

### Cited Findings
- Statistics Canada’s 2024 CMA table reports overall crime rates of 3,674 per 100,000 in Montréal and 4,177 in Toronto; its Crime Severity Index values are 61.7 and 59.4, respectively. These are all-crime indicators, not residential burglary/theft rates, and are affected by policing practices and police-service boundaries. [Statistics Canada, 2024 Crime Severity Index by CMA](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/250722/t004a-eng.htm)
- Statistics Canada defines these CMA crime-rate denominators as population and notes a CMA can include multiple municipalities/police services; its notes explicitly warn police-reported statistics can vary based on how police services process offences. These data therefore do not directly represent insured homes, policy exposures, or claim propensity. [Statistics Canada table notes](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/250722/t004a-eng.htm)
- Statistics Canada’s 2026 national insurance analysis identifies increased auto theft as one factor influencing driver insurance costs, while its Home discussion emphasizes flood and property/replacement cost factors. This distinction reinforces separating Auto theft signals from Home theft claims. [Statistics Canada, Extreme weather impacts on consumers and insurers, 2019–2025](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11-621-m/11-621-m2026007-eng.htm)

### Inferences
- For Home theft: obtain loss-cause detail (burglary, contents theft, vandalism, attempted theft), address-level exposure, occupancy/protection features where legally and appropriately collected, insured value, coverage and deductible. Compare insured claims per property-year with local police break-and-enter data only as contextual triangulation; do not use overall crime-rate differences as an explanation.
- Fire has a distinct rare/severe profile in the supplied 13-claim category (provided by the coordinating analysis). Analyze fire separately with long-period, mature ultimate losses and catastrophe/event clustering; a one-year slice is too sparse for stable regional conclusions.

### Gaps
- No retrieved official source here provides 2024 Montréal- and Toronto-specific residential break-and-enter rates that align to the insurance portfolio’s geography and timeframe.
- No external evidence reviewed establishes that cultural/ethnic composition or age causes the portfolio’s property claims. If household or demographic data are considered, they need a lawful, necessary purpose, consent/privacy review, fairness review, and must not become unsupported proxies for claims risk.

## How should external explanations be tested rather than assumed?

### Takeaway

The credible approach is a staged, falsifiable investigation: name the anomaly from the portfolio, enumerate competing mechanisms, add comparable exposure and local data, then measure whether a mechanism survives adjustment and out-of-time validation. A narrative or article is a lead for a test, not proof of causation.

### Cited Findings
- ECCC’s Historical Climate Data service provides hourly, daily and monthly weather observations, station metadata, precipitation and temperature summaries, and climate normals; it can provide time-matched weather covariates if locations/stations are selected transparently. [ECCC Historical Climate Data](https://climate.weather.gc.ca/)
- Natural Resources Canada’s Flood Hazard Identification and Mapping Program develops flood hazard information and mapping; federal assessment notes flood maps have historically been inconsistent/outdated and recent efforts aim to improve them, so map provenance, model date, hazard type and spatial resolution must be retained. [NRCan Flood Hazard Identification and Mapping Program](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/science-data/science-research/natural-hazards/flood-mapping/flood-hazard-identification-mapping-program); [Canada’s Changing Climate Report 2026, Chapter 5](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/science-technology/changing-climate-report-2026/cccr-chapter-5-en.html)
- Research using a British Columbia insurer’s linked claims, property and high-resolution weather dataset reports precipitation effects in that portfolio primarily through claim frequency, and identifies the need for property/weather linkage. It is a preprint/working paper and is specific to one BC insurer; its reported effect sizes must not be transferred to Montréal or Toronto. [Weather Impacts on Home Insurance Losses: Evidence from British Columbia](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/5373387.pdf?abstractid=5373387&mirid=1)

### Inferences
- Build an evidence ledger for each observation: exact portfolio rows/date window/denominator; the hypothesized mechanism; evidence that supports it; evidence against it; alternate explanations (mix, exposure, coverage, coding, severity, reporting lag); dataset needed next; and a falsification rule. Preserve source geography, sample, dates, methods, and URL so the user can inspect why an explanation was considered.
- For the Montréal–Toronto comparison, use staged tests: (1) normalize labels and deduplicate claims; (2) compare peril mix and calendar/event month; (3) calculate exposure-adjusted frequency, severity, and pure premium; (4) standardize for product, limits, deductible, property type/age and tenure; (5) add weather/flood layers; (6) quantify uncertainty and validate on a later period. Avoid causal wording unless the design supports it.
- If external data are unavailable or sparse, the product should explicitly say “we need this data to test this explanation” and prioritize the data collection that separates plausible mechanisms. Do not manufacture a city cause, demographic profile, or profit result from aggregate data.

### Gaps
- A validated Montreal–Toronto city-cause comparison requires portfolio geocodes and sufficient exposure years; no public article can substitute for those proprietary records.
- Weather/event association alone is not proof of attribution: property-level vulnerability, coverage, insured values, claim reporting, and policy selection can also shape observed claims.
