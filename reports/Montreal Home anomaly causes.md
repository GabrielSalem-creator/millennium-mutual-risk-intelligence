# Investigate Montreal’s Home Insurance Claim Gap

The supplied files show a **large Montreal–Toronto difference in Home claim records per policy row**: Montreal has 115 claims across 510 Home policy rows (22.5 per 100), while Toronto has 79 across 570 (13.9 per 100), a raw ratio of **1.63×**. Montreal’s Home records also contain more Water Damage claims; Toronto has more Wind/Hail claims. This is a high-value anomaly to investigate, not evidence that Montreal itself caused the difference: coverage end dates, insured-years, location, property characteristics, and ultimate losses are missing. External evidence offers plausible mechanisms in both cities, but none establishes the cause in this portfolio. The correct next move is to gather exposure and peril detail, test competing explanations, and use prevention or pricing strategies only when the adjusted evidence supports them.

## Montreal’s Home claim count is higher, but exposure is unknown

After removing 15 exact duplicate claim rows, the file contains 847 unique claims. The Home subset contains 283 claims in total. Montreal has 115 claims from 510 policy rows; Toronto has 79 from 570. The corresponding record counts per policy row are 0.2255 and 0.1386. Montreal’s recorded claim amount totals 934,271.22 units and Toronto’s 698,398.94; the currency is not identified. Mean reported claim amount is 8,267.89 in Montreal and 8,840.49 in Toronto. Thus the higher Montreal total is mainly a claim-count signal in this extract, not higher average severity. These are file-level descriptions, not earned-exposure rates, ultimate costs, or profit estimates.

The peril mix is more informative than the city total: Montreal records 79 Water Damage, 19 Theft, 14 Wind/Hail, and 3 Fire claims; Toronto records 28 Water Damage, 21 Theft, 24 Wind/Hail, and 6 Fire claims. The signal could reflect water exposure or property mix, but it could also reflect policy tenure, coverage, deductible, claim coding, reporting, or chance. Calendar-year claim counts are 46 Montreal versus 34 Toronto in 2025, and 65 versus 44 in 2026 through the latest loss date in the extract (October 30). The 2024 comparison is too thin to interpret: Montreal has one Home claim and Toronto none. Without policy end/cancellation dates, a “claims per policy” comparison cannot tell whether Montreal policies were insured longer.

## Flood pathways are plausible in both cities, not a proven cause

Both municipal sources describe mechanisms consistent with Home water losses. Montréal identifies intense rainfall, sewer overload and backup, low-lying areas, and basement entry points; it recommends measures such as backwater valves and sump pumps. Toronto also lists heavy rainfall and snowmelt, overloaded sewers, groundwater, foundation and drainage issues, and sewer-line blockages. This supports investigating water-cause subtype, property vulnerability, and storm timing in both cities; it does not predict which insurer portfolio should have more claims ([Montréal heavy-rain guidance](https://montreal.ca/en/articles/protecting-your-building-heavy-rains-68382), [Toronto basement-flooding guidance](https://www.toronto.ca/services-payments/water-environment/managing-rain-melted-snow/basement-flooding/)).

Climate evidence strengthens the case for testing weather as a factor, but not for assigning blame. Canada’s 2026 climate assessment says annual precipitation has increased and is projected to increase, while confidence in observed changes to heavy-precipitation frequency and intensity is lower than for future projections. Its results are national and do not explain this dataset’s Montreal–Toronto difference. Statistics Canada reports substantial flood-related insured losses nationally, including $9 billion paid over the prior decade with nearly half in 2024; this is a market context figure, not a loss estimate for these policies ([Canada’s Changing Climate Report 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/science-technology/changing-climate-report-2026/cccr-chapter-5-en.html), [Statistics Canada, insurer and consumer impacts](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11-621-m/11-621-m2026007-eng.htm)).

The evidence also argues against a one-factor story. Statistics Canada’s 2024 CMA figures show Toronto’s overall police-reported crime rate above Montréal’s (4,177 versus 3,674 per 100,000), while Montréal’s Crime Severity Index is slightly higher (61.7 versus 59.4). Neither measure is a residential burglary rate or an insured Home claim rate, so these indicators cannot explain the portfolio’s Theft counts ([Statistics Canada 2024 Crime Severity Index by CMA](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/250722/t004a-eng.htm)). The data show 19 Montreal Home Theft claims versus 21 in Toronto, so Home theft does not drive Montreal’s higher claim count in this sample. External research should be recorded as evidence for or against a hypothesis, with its geography, period, outcome definition, and limitations intact.

## Test competing explanations before choosing a strategy

Start with a falsifiable comparison. First, reconstruct insured property-years by policy and coverage effective/termination dates, and reconcile multiple claims per policy and customers holding successive policies. Then compare Montreal and Toronto by accident year, month/event, peril subtype, property type/age, construction, basement, protection devices, insured value, coverage form, limits, and deductible. Use stable territory definitions and location privacy controls. A frequency model should use earned exposure as its denominator (for example, Poisson or negative-binomial counts with log exposure as an offset); severity should be modeled separately on mature, ultimate claim costs. Standardize the cities to a shared portfolio mix, quantify uncertainty, and validate against a later period. If the difference vanishes after matching exposure or property mix, the city label was not the actionable cause; if it persists and aligns with a verifiable peril, target prevention or underwriting review to that peril.

The best external additions are specific and matchable. Environment and Climate Change Canada offers historical hourly, daily, and monthly weather observations and climate normals; join loss dates to transparently selected station/grid data and retain station coverage and missingness ([ECCC Historical Climate Data](https://climate.weather.gc.ca/)). Natural Resources Canada’s flood-susceptibility maps can screen areas, but NRCan says they are for regional/national screening rather than individual-property prediction or engineering decisions; map year, hazard type, resolution, and provenance must travel with every result ([NRCan historic flood susceptibility](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/ea1384df-bf4a-4743-97bb-870dc43f8d77), [NRCan intended-use notes](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/1074f781-85d3-4c86-86cb-fd1c339197dc)). Statistics Canada housing-stock data can contextualize neighborhood mix, but it is not building-level policy data and its census boundaries need crosswalks ([2021 Census Profile](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/en/catalogue/98-401-X)). Public crime data is context only. IBC catastrophe summaries and OSFI company returns can benchmark broad market conditions, but do not provide the portfolio’s municipality-level insured exposures and claim development ([IBC data hub](https://www.ibc.ca/industry-resources/industry-impact-and-data-hub), [OSFI P&C open returns](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/4c6c515f-7c44-4bb6-ab56-c5f79f75f705)).

The app should maintain an evidence ledger per observation: exact dataset slice and denominator; observed pattern; possible mechanisms; evidence supporting and challenging each; alternative explanations; missing fields; source geography/time/method; next test; and a rule that could disprove the hypothesis. Where evidence is insufficient, it should say exactly which data are needed rather than fill the gap with an article or a plausible-sounding narrative. For this anomaly, the highest-value request is coverage start/end and cancellation dates, insured postal area, property age/type and basement/plumbing/mitigation details, water-cause subtypes, limits/deductibles, claim event identifiers, reserve snapshots, and paid/incurred development.

## Profit needs earned premium and ultimate losses

The policy file’s annual premium is written premium, not necessarily the amount earned during the claim period. A loss ratio uses incurred losses divided by earned premium; reported and paid amounts alone are incomplete because outstanding case estimates, development on reported claims, incurred-but-not-reported claims (IBNR), and loss-adjustment expense also matter ([NAIC insurance glossary](https://content.naic.org/glossary-insurance-terms?id=37), [Canadian Institute of Actuaries P&C standards](https://www.cia-ica.ca/app/themes/wicket/custom/dl_file.php?fid=32707&p=37243)). The profit bridge therefore needs matched-period earned premium and exposure, ultimate net claims, LAE, commissions and acquisition/admin expenses, reinsurance premium and recoveries, investment income, and currency. Until then, the reason profits are low is not calculable from these CSVs.

For long-run loss, let aggregate claims be (S=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_i), with claim count (N) and severity (X_i). Under comparable exposure and suitable independence, expected aggregate loss can be represented as expected frequency times expected severity. The Law of Large Numbers helps stabilize averages only as relevant exposure accumulates; more policy rows do not automatically stabilize losses when coverage periods differ, risk mix changes, or catastrophe claims are correlated. Chebyshev’s bound, (P(|\bar X-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)), requires a defensible variance, denominator, and dependence assumption. Credibility blending such as (Z=N/(N+K)) likewise needs exposure and within/between-group variability; this extract cannot supply a valid (K). These are tools for quantified uncertainty, not a shortcut from the observed city ratio to a price change ([OSFI MCT Guideline 2026](https://www.osfi-bsif.gc.ca/en/guidance/guidance-library/minimum-capital-test-guideline-2026), [CAS credibility review](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/2021-03/8_Fisher_et_al.pdf)).

## Turn findings into measured decisions

The present signal supports an investigation and possibly a controlled loss-prevention pilot—not immediate city repricing. If exposure-adjusted Montreal water frequency remains higher after matching property and coverage, test targeted basement-water mitigation with a comparison group and measure take-up, claims per insured property-year, ultimate severity, customer outcomes, and program cost. If the excess is mostly one event or reporting/coding difference, update catastrophe accumulation, claim coding, or data-quality controls rather than individual prices. If higher frequency is offset by lower severity or adequate earned premium, a price increase could damage retention without improving margin. If matched loss costs exceed earned premium after expenses and credibility is sufficient, prepare a segment-level, actuarially reviewed rate action in the applicable province and product.

Do not substitute cultural, ethnic, age, mortality, or hospital-use statistics for missing property data. Such variables do not explain building water damage and can act as discriminatory proxies; aggregate neighborhood characteristics also do not describe an individual policyholder. For Home losses, collect and test direct mechanisms—property features, insured location, peril, coverage, mitigation, exposure, and claim development. Any fairness audit that uses sensitive attributes needs a separate lawful and privacy-reviewed purpose, not an underwriting shortcut ([CAS paper on indirect discrimination in insurance rates](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/2025-10/_A_Scalable_toolbox_working_paper.pdf)).

## Conclusion

The portfolio has a compelling Montreal Home anomaly, especially in Water Damage, but it has not yet shown a Montreal risk rate or a causal city effect. The most valuable next data are policy-level insured time and location/property/peril detail; those fields can separate exposure, portfolio mix, weather, and preventable loss. Profit analysis should then combine ultimate claims with earned premium and full expenses, quantify uncertainty, and gate each strategy on a matched comparison. The system should preserve this distinction at every stage: observed signal, competing explanation, evidence, missing data, decision, and measured result.
